Session 1: from effect estimates to a rule a caseworker can apply. The lab is AI-off.

Two ways to allocate

A ranking by estimated benefit is hard to explain; a short rule can be read at the counter

Ranking

Top fifth by a causal forest's estimate. Each seeker's place depends on 14 attributes and 400 trees.

Rule

At most two questions about a seeker, each one a threshold. A caseworker can apply it, and a seeker can be told why.

Day 3 showed a hand-written rule on months unemployed matched the ranking's value. Today the rule is learned, not guessed.

Ask: what would you need to tell a seeker who is refused a place? With a rule, one sentence. With a ranking, nothing you could defend.

The gain of each seeker

Doubly robust scores are noisy for one seeker and unbiased for any group

score = mu1 - mu0 + t * (y - mu1) / e - (1 - t) * (y - mu0) / (1 - e)   # e = 0.5 in the pilot
8.8 points
mean score on the training split: the average effect
92 points
standard deviation of one seeker's score

From the course's run of Lab 4.1. The same score as Day 1's DML, kept per seeker instead of averaged. A tree sums scores over a leaf, so the noise averages out; a ranking of single scores would be useless. Source: Athey and Wager, Policy learning with observational data.

Policy trees

Search every pair of splits; keep the tree whose offered seekers have the largest summed gain

Split 1every allowed attribute, every threshold
→
Split 2the best split inside each side
→
Leavesoffer where the summed score beats the cost
Unlike a CART tree, it is not greedy: the first split is chosen for what the second makes possible.

Depth two over six attributes and 12 thresholds is about 5,000 trees; exhaustive search is cheap at this size and impossible much beyond depth three. The course's U.policy_tree mirrors the R package policytree.

One place per five seekers

A budget becomes a cost per place: raise it until the tree offers at most 20%

for _ in range(12):
    cost = (lo + hi) / 2
    t = U.policy_tree(X, g, cost=cost)
    if U.predict_tree(t, X).mean() <= 0.2:
        best, hi = (t, cost), cost
    else:
        lo = cost
17 points
the cost per place at which the tree fits the budget

A seeker earns a place only where coaching is expected to raise her chance of work by more than 17 points.

The cost is the shadow price of a place: what the last place is worth. It is also a number a director can understand: "we coach where coaching adds at least 17 points".

Session 2: which attributes may decide, how to audit, and what a fairness constraint costs. This is where the data protection officer joins the discussion.

Before learning any rule

Every attribute gets a role: drive the rule, only audit it, or stay out

Drive

Months unemployed, digital skills, education, prior jobs, licence, distance: registered facts a caseworker can explain.

Audit

Region, gender, disability, age: never used to decide; every result is checked across them.

Exclude

Prior wage, dependants, the motivation rating, the risk score.

Shuruq Al-Obaikan, the DPO, asks for this table before any model is fitted. The course's choices are defensible, not the only ones: prior wage carries past pay gaps; dependants were collected for benefits; motivation is a subjective rating seekers would learn to game; the risk score is built partly from age and disability.

Checkpoint

What does an equal selection rate prove?

An audit shows a rule offers places to disabled and non-disabled seekers at the same rate. A board member concludes the rule is fair to disabled seekers. What do you add?

Nothing: equal rates are the definition of fairness
The rule should use disability to offer more places
Equal rates say who is offered, not who gains; disabled seekers may gain more per place, which a choice to prioritise them would weigh
Equal rates show the rule ignores disability, which is discrimination
Why. Selection rates are one view; gains by group are another. Disabled seekers truly gain 26 points per place against 21; deciding whether that justifies priority is a policy choice.

Common problems: putting age in the drive list without a reason; reading a selection-rate ratio of 0.91 as unfair (it is a number to report, against a threshold the agency sets); comparing the national and floor values with separate intervals. The free-attribute tree takes about 30 seconds.

Session 3: the offline value is a hypothesis. A trial confirms it, and sizing the trial is where most plans fail.

What the trial compares

Randomise seekers to policies, not to coaching

Arm A

The depth-two rule decides whether the seeker is offered a place.

Arm B

Current practice decides: the caseworker, with the allocation tool.

Estimand. The difference in employment within six months between the arms, among all randomised seekers (intention to treat).

The pilot already showed coaching works. The question now is whether deciding by the rule beats deciding as today. An offline value, however careful, is the hypothesis; this trial is the test.

Lab 4.3, sizing

A 1.4-point difference needs about 18,000 seekers per arm: nearly two years of registrations

18,246
seekers per arm, 5% two-sided, 80% power
1.8 years
of Fursa's registrations, about 19,900 a year
1,328

The disagreeing seekers cannot be identified in advance, because the caseworker's decision is not known until it is made; the last number shows what dilution costs. Ask: what could shorten the trial? A larger difference (a better rule), a more sensitive outcome, or pooling several agencies.

Seekers or offices?

Randomising Fursa's 11 offices would multiply the sample by 119

Offices

No mixing of policies inside an office, but the design effect 1 + (m − 1) ICC with ICC 0.036 and thousands of seekers per office is 119.

Seekers, stratified by office

Feasible. Contamination is checked by caseworker compliance with the assigned policy.

What neither design sees: displacement. Crépon and colleagues found job-search help for some seekers partly at the expense of others in the same labour markets.

Source: Crépon, Duflo, Gurgand, Rathelot and Zamora, Do labor market policies have displacement effects? (2013). Measuring displacement needs randomisation at the level of labour markets, with varying shares treated: far beyond one agency.

Lab 4.3, one interim look

O'Brien–Fleming boundaries spend almost nothing at the halfway look

|z| > 2.79
to stop at half the seekers
|z| > 1.97
at the end, instead of 1.96

Found by simulating pairs of correlated z-statistics until the chance of crossing either boundary under no difference is 5%. Simulated power with 1% more seekers: about 80%.

Looking twice at 1.96 would push the false-positive rate to about 8%. The boundary at the halfway look is strict enough that the trial stops early only for a large difference, or for harm.

Checkpoint

Why is the trial so large when the rule finds twice the jobs per place?

The rule finds 20.7 jobs per 100 places against current practice's 11.4, yet the trial needs about 18,000 seekers per arm. Why?

The per-place gain is too small to detect
Only one seeker in six gets a place in either arm, and the arms agree on most seekers, so the difference across all seekers is 1.4 points
The interim look doubles the sample size
Offices are clustered, so the design effect inflates the sample
Why. The trial compares everyone in one arm with everyone in the other; a large gain for a few seekers is a small difference for the whole population.

Answer: B. Dilution is the most common reason trials of targeting rules are underpowered.

Common problems: sizing for the per-place gain instead of the population difference; computing the ICC from seekers instead of office rates; treating the design effect as optional. Early finishers: simulate the trial.
