Logged decisions

The recorded p is the key. When it is right, no propensity model is needed, and rules never used can be valued.

Fursa's allocation tool scored each seeker and offered coaching with a probability rather than by a hard cut-off; that randomness is what makes off-policy evaluation possible. Many real allocation systems log no probability at all; then you are back to Day 1's estimated propensities.

The baseline every rule must beat

Current practice needs no weights: its value is the employment rate in the logs

57.6%
employed within six months, 40,000 decisions (Lab 3.1)
  • The logs were produced by current practice.
  • Their mean outcome is its value, with a standard error of 0.2 points.
  • Every candidate rule is compared with this number.

Without RUM-2 the value is 57.5%; the difference comes from that office's higher offer rate. Ask: is current practice a rule? Yes: a stochastic one, partly written in the tool and partly in caseworkers' heads.

A check that costs one line

If the recorded probabilities are right, the weights a/p and (1-a)/(1-p) average to 1

w1 = a / p
w0 = (1 - a) / (1 - p)
print(w1.mean(), w0.mean())

Fursa's logs

Mean of a/p: 1.148, standard error 0.019. Eight standard errors from 1.

The other weight

Mean of (1-a)/(1-p): 0.976, standard error 0.003.

From the course's run of Lab 3.1. Both are off: more offers happened than the records predict. This is the simplest test of a logging system, and it is rarely run. Ask: where would you look next? By office, by period, by group.

What to do with a broken record

Exclude the office or re-estimate its propensities, and say which

Exclude

Lose 10% of the data; every other office's records pass. The course's choice.

Re-estimate

Keep

Checkpoint

What does a mean of a/p equal to 1.15 tell you?

In an agency's logs, the mean of offered divided by the recorded probability is 1.15, with a standard error of 0.02. What follows?

Coaching raised employment by 15%
More offers happened than recorded: some probabilities are too low
Nothing: weights are noisy and a mean of 1.15 is normal
The tool records probabilities that are too high
Why. With correct probabilities, a/p averages to 1. Above 1 means offers were more frequent than recorded, so recorded p is too small somewhere.

Answer: B. If many pick D, write E[a] = p on the board: a/p averages to (true rate)/(recorded p).

02

Session 2: three estimators of a rule's value, and the first value table of the week.

Inverse propensity value

Keep the decisions the rule agrees with, and weight each by one over its probability

p_a = np.where(a == 1, p, 1 - p)     # probability of what the logs did
w   = (a == rule) / p_a               # zero where the rule disagrees
value = np.mean(w * y)
Recap from DS-303. The same weights estimated an average effect there; a rule's value is the weighted mean outcome under one decision per seeker.

Walk through one decision: a seeker the logs offered with p = 0.25, whom the rule would also offer, counts with weight 4. A seeker the logs did not offer and the rule would offer counts zero: we do not see what coaching would have done for her. Sources: Dudík, Langford and Li, Doubly robust policy evaluation and learning.

Two refinements

Self-normalising tames the weights; augmenting adds an outcome model and keeps the estimate honest

Self-normalised

Divide by the sum of the weights, not by n. A little bias, much less variance.

Augmented

Start from μ under the rule; add the weighted residual where logs and rule agree. Consistent if either part is right.

psi = np.where(rule == 1, mu1, mu0) + w * (y - np.where(a == 1, mu1, mu0))

The augmented score is Day 1's DML score turned towards a rule: an outcome-model guess, corrected by weighted residuals. The supplied mu0 and mu1 were cross-fitted on the logs, so no decision's own outcome predicts itself.

Checkpoint

When is the augmented value still right?

You value a rule with the augmented estimator. The recorded probabilities have been checked and are right; the outcome model is poor. What happens?

The estimate is biased, because the outcome model is wrong
The estimate is right and as precise as with a good model
It stays consistent, with a wider interval
The estimate equals the IPW value exactly
Why. With correct weights the residual term corrects any outcome model on average; a poor model leaves larger residuals, so more variance.

Answer: C. This is double robustness: correct if either part is right, most precise when both are close.

A rule from outside

"Reach the unreached": coach the groups the logs almost never coached

  • Disabled seekers in Rumh and Nijad.

A fair question

The rule comes from an advocacy group. Its aim is legitimate; the question is whether the logs can value it.

Be careful with tone: the rule is not foolish, and Day 4's fairness session takes its concern seriously. The point here is narrower: the logs hold almost no evidence about coaching these groups.

Here plain IPW lands near the truth; that is luck you cannot see in real data. The diagnosis is about trust, not about this one number: the estimate depends on a few decisions, and clipping, which adds unknown bias, moves it. The augmented value leans on an outcome model that saw few coached seekers like these.

A checklist for every value you report

Report the effective sample size, the heaviest weights and the clipped values with every rule's value

DiagnosticWarning sign
Share of rule-treated seekers the logs treatedUnder one in ten

The thresholds are rules of thumb, not standards: say so. The remedy for an unevaluable rule is data: a small randomised pilot with the groups in question. That is the answer to the advocacy group.

Checkpoint

What do you tell the advocacy group?

The "reach the unreached" rule has an IPW value 1.6 points below current practice, an effective sample size of 58 among the seekers it would coach, and moves by a point when weights are clipped. What do you recommend?

Reject the rule: the logs show it adds nothing
Report the value clipped at 20 as the estimate
Say the logs cannot value it; propose a small pilot
Report the augmented value, which does not depend on the weights
Why. The estimate rests on a few heavy weights and an extrapolating outcome model; only new data on these seekers can answer the question.

Answer: C. Option D is the subtle trap: the augmented estimator does depend on the weights, and where weights are huge it leans on mu1 for seekers the outcome model barely saw.

Session 4

04

Session 4 is a review exercise: reading someone else's heterogeneous-effect claim the way a reviewer, a regulator or an auditor would.

A draft on the director's desk

An analyst found who benefits most, and recommends whom to coach first

1. Seekers who attended four or more sessions gain 27.0 points.

2. Men aged 40 to 44 in Sahil with secondary education gain 49.5 points (n = 36, p = 0.001).

3. 8 of 60 cells had p < 0.05: the targets. In the others coaching showed no effect.

4. Predicted and observed effects agree across deciles: correlation above 0.9.

The draft is labs/data/review/anwar_analysis.md, by Anwar Al-Mohaimeed. Read it aloud. Ask the room to vote: which finding would you trust most? Then spend the session showing that none survives.

Fault 1

A subgroup defined after the lottery is selected, not randomised

3.42 vs 2.50
motivation before the lottery: four or more sessions against fewer
  • Only coached seekers have sessions; no comparable uncoached group exists.
  • Attenders were more motivated before anything happened.
  • The randomised effect for everyone offered is 10.6 points, not 27.0.

From the course's run of Lab 3.4. Sessions attended is a post-treatment variable: it may be affected by coaching itself and by the same traits that drive employment. The honest question about attendance needs another design: principal stratification, or encouraging attendance at random.

Fault 2

The largest of 60 cells is mostly the noise that made it the largest

The analyst's cell, claimed
49.5
The same cell, true effect
9.7
Honest search: top five, training
23.3
The same five, held out
12.4

Points of employment. Discover on one split, evaluate on another.

The analyst searched all 6,000 seekers, so his cell cannot be re-tested on held-out seekers: they were part of the search. The truth (9.7 points) is the generator's. Searching on the training split and testing on the held-out split shows the general pattern: winners shrink by about half. The national bias guide calls this data-dredging bias.

Fault 3

Eight significant cells out of sixty do not pick out the targets, and silence is not absence

The targets

8 cells at p < 0.05; after a Bonferroni correction, 2. Sixty tests at 0.05 give three by chance alone, even if coaching did nothing.

The "no effect" cells

Their true effect averages 11.3 points: cells of 30 to 60 seekers cannot show it.

Recap from DS-302 Session 4.2 on many segments. Both halves of the finding are wrong: the significant cells are partly noise, and the insignificant ones are underpowered, not empty. Reporting all 60 with corrected intervals would have shown this.

Fault 4

A model checked on the seekers it was fitted to agrees with itself

0.99
decile correlation, fitted and checked on all seekers
0.81
fitted on training, checked on held-out seekers
The held-out number is still good. The fault is not that the model is useless; it is that the evidence offered for it proves nothing.

From the course's run of Lab 3.4, with the course's lightly regularised boosting as the analyst's model. The national bias guide's name for judging a model on data unlike its use is evaluation bias.

The review memo

Name each fault, show its evidence, state its effect on the claim

FaultEvidenceEffect on the claim
Post-treatment subgroupMotivation 3.42 against 2.5027 points mixes coaching with motivation; randomised effect 11

The memo is the evidence of LO5. Tone: the analyst is a colleague, and every fault here is common. A good memo also says what would make the claim credible: pre-registered subgroups, a split for discovery and one for evaluation, all cells reported with corrected intervals.

Checkpoint

Which fix answers the dredged cell?

A colleague wants to keep the analyst's best cell but make it credible. Which step does that?

Report the p-value to three decimals so readers see it is 0.001
Search more cells to show the finding is robust
Bootstrap the cell's 36 seekers and report the interval
Test the cell on seekers outside the search
Why. Only data not used in the search can estimate the effect of a cell without the inflation that selection added.

Answer: D. In practice: Fursa's 2027 cohort, or a randomised pilot. The capstone brief for coaching has exactly such a cohort.
